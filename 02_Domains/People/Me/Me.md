## Background

When I was 15 or 16, I was recommended a suite of books by the Portuguese neuroscientist Antonio Damasio. The book reaches into ideas around homeostasis, and how this highly evolved precision system that is so effective at modelling the external environment must also be able to model how the environment changes its internal state, and this recursive self-modelling eventually leads to a sense of self.

There are various parallels of second-order perception, awareness of awareness, and its relationship to consciousness that are now cornerstones of AI thinkers like Joscha Bach, and there are roots there in the Free Energy Principle and Bayesian brain, which I don't believe Damasio was thinking of at the time, but this idea of homeostasis and recursive self-modelling left a mark on me at the time. I was also heavily drawn to this idea of the brain as more of a distributed than modular system, which came to shape some of my thinking today.

But more than any of that, what I really loved about the book was the way Damasio linked together literature, philosophy, and art. He talked about Ulysses, my favourite novel at the time, and Shakespeare, and you could see that the way he thought about human behaviour was just as shaped by the arts as by science.

Both my parents are artists. My father, Raul Alejandro Vinao, is a composer, and my mother, Lachele Jeana Carl, is an actress, and I grew up playing piano from a young age. I first started classes at age four and had a music scholarship to City of London School for Boys, a private school, from the age of ten. Although I was rewarded for my excellent GCSE grades, 11 A* and 1 A, with an academic scholarship, I would still say honestly that I was one of those students that did well in exams and was not a natural thinker when it came to the sciences. For instance, I was downgraded from advanced maths to regular maths for my A levels, which was too difficult for me.

I was not ever a naturally "gifted" scientist. I love science, but at school I would always say it took me longer to grasp concepts than your average student. I was smart, maybe above average, and I had to work ten times harder than most people, at least from my perspective, to achieve similar grades to what it appeared to me other students were able to do with ease.

But when it came to the arts I had a voracious appetite for reading. I was part of a small cabal of bookworms that had a pact to read 200 pages every day, and I read all the classics: Byron, Joyce, T.S. Eliot, Tennyson, Robert Musil, Dostoevsky, and Tolstoy, quite literally everything. And I was a stellar student. So I was immediately seduced by this book that appeared to show me that the arts, something I was naturally good at, might be useful in some non-obvious way to the sciences, something I found more difficult, and it made me feel that maybe someone like me could have a place in science after all.

## Scientific Identity

What I mean by "computational neuroscientist" is somebody who uses data-driven methods to learn behavioural representations from neural data and use these to understand the mechanisms driving human behaviour and derive predictive models to track changes in human states. Although the work has changed substantially over the years, I have always prided myself as someone who likes to take strategies from different disciplines and apply them to my work. For instance, I am convinced that the brain is a fluid dynamic system that should be represented as a superposition of different waves rather than as a network, and all of the mathematical tools I used to describe this fluid-like or wave-like system were derived from physics and fluid mechanics, for which I had no prior training or knowledge of.

I enjoy this kind of cross-domain problem solving immensely. I would say that is a core part of my identity, as well as wanting to always understand the mechanisms underlying human behaviour and thought. I don't want to just be a prediction insect. I want to take things apart and understand how the pieces fit together and use this to understand how all the rich and complex behaviours that define who we are, and all the experiences you read in a novel, arise from this dense complex network of meat that we call a brain.

I felt that the field of neuroscience was at a crossroads where we were starting to have increasingly powerful and precise perturbation tools for controlling and modulating circuit activity in humans for the first time, but lacked computational frameworks to identify the circuits and functional brain networks underpinning disease and behaviour. Over the next two years I became increasingly convinced that the types of behaviours we are interested in in humans, attention, memory, are large-scale distributed processes that cannot be captured by any single brain region or oscillatory process, and that the field needed to develop more holistic systems spatiotemporal frameworks for thinking about cognition. This eventually led to my discovery of these highly conserved networks of travelling waves which underlie various aspects of human behaviour and cognition.

For various reasons I did not end up staying in academia, many of which are related to career progression and the lab dynamics at Imperial, but one of the scientific reasons was that I felt that I had found a real actionable substrate for human cognition which now could be coupled with perturbation tools like TI to modulate human behaviour, but that we lacked a principled way to read out and define human behaviour. For instance, I could not understand how the vast behavioural complexities of Alzheimer's disease could be adequately defined by choice response tasks and face-name paradigms.

It seems clear from interacting with anyone with an NDD that the condition is a brain-wide and body-wide phenomenon that affects gait and posture and language, it affects memory and perception and motor control, it affects mood and the endocrine system and homeostasis, and we did not have ways to measure or model these, let alone predict how these were changing over time.

## How I Think

I usually start with some intuitive understanding of a process that lives in an abstract concept space, and later give weight to this and substantiate it through mathematics. For instance, I would always visualise EEG signals from different conditions, rest vs task, AD vs HCs, and make time-resolved videos of the activity and look for differences qualitatively.

I noticed that the activity would tend to form eddy currents, swirls that looked rotational, source and sink flow that looked like waveguides from electromagnetism. At the time I didn't know that there was already a field called Neural Field Theory and there was a booming field of travelling waves in neuroscience, but everyone around me was a connectionist modelling the brain as a graph, and this just seemed absurd to me. I could not understand how anyone looking at neural activity could see a graph, which could only be a second-order representation of the thing we are seeing and not generative. A graph isn't the reason why neural systems behave the way they do, it is just an emergent description of the activity. But wave equations and Navier–Stokes are fundamental, derived from first principles, so a model of the brain in terms of fluid flows seemed far richer and more complete.

More recently, I tend to think in terms of dynamical systems trying to minimise entropy production, and to do this they require modelling themselves and their environment to minimise surprise, and this self-modelling probably occurs in some shared latent space that maybe can be described by a basis of flow modes like the ones I discovered, but I'm sure is much more complex, like the embedding spaces we see in AI models, and multimodal, joint embedding spaces that are not just derived from electrophysiological or BOLD signals but also neuromodulator and neuroendocrine systems, as well as all the other signalling molecules inside and outside the body that we cannot measure with EEG.

My epistemological style is more nuanced and context-dependent than any single formula. I don't want to be constrained to thinking about this phenomenologically and deriving mathematical ideas only from abstract concepts/intuition. I do often like to work backwards, for instance noticing that certain computational methods apply to other dynamical systems, like a fluid, and positing that the brain is itself a physical system that must obey certain laws, e.g. thermodynamics, and hence reasoning that there may be similar mathematics, like fluid mechanics, that underlies them both. I don't want this to be reductive or to constrain the creative process in the long run.

It's also worth noting that for some problems, like developing EEG insights, I may need to develop tooling that is purely prediction-based and not mechanistic-based. After all, there are many examples in the history of science where we developed interventions without fully understanding mechanisms, e.g. SSRIs. I aspire to understand how things work at a more fundamental level, but I understand that there is value in developing models, e.g. if doing so helps us scale up data collection in order to build better mechanistic models.

## Goals

I want to go back to this core feeling of curiosity as a teenager about how this lump of meat gives us consciousness, how we can fall in love and dance tango and do all the things that cannot be measured in pen-and-paper clinical assessments. But I also want to feel useful to others around me. I want to work in collaborative multidisciplinary teams. I want to feel like there is a synergy and feeling of emergence, that the work we do together is more than the sum of its parts, but also that without me there the startup machine wouldn't function well either. So I try to put myself in difficult environments with people that are much smarter than I am so that I can learn, but I also want to feel that I have a sense of purpose by being useful to others. I will quit if I feel like I am a burden on the team.

On the cosmic timescales of my life, I want to found a lab with a brother/sister PI that studies whole-body embodied cognition using multimodal imaging, and to understand the mechanisms underpinning extreme states of consciousness, DMT, sleep, consciousness in children, insects, sense of self, the ego, jhanas, meditative states, and how these arise from the brain and body and what are the critical elements for these states.

To do that I need to stay at the frontiers of computation and software engineering, because most progress in science, I believe, comes from technological innovation, and we are in the midst of an AI revolution that cannot be ignored, and I want to understand the limits of AI and where we can use AI to understand human cognition and emotions and consciousness.

I am very concerned with the way I, and we, are using AI today, that we use these tools to export critical thinking and prompt these tools to do all the heavy cognitive lifting, and that it is eroding our ability to think, and I want to use these tools in a healthy constructive way to accelerate and realise my potential and help me learn new skills and appreciate and incorporate new technologies and scientific theories into my thinking without conceding what it means to be human, that is, our capacity to be creative and to think critically.

So I see our relationship as a partnership that can help me grow as a person and scientist and partner to my, one day, wife, but I want everything I say and do to be my own, to feel that I have ownership over my ideas and not to be robbed of the joy of using my mind.
