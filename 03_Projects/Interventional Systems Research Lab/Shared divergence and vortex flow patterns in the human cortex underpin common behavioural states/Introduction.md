Identifying common representations of neural activity that reflect the same underlying brain process across recordings is a central goal of systems neuroscience[1](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?PFueJF). Solving this correspondence problem is essential for comparing healthy and disease states, or for distinguishing between behavioral states such as wakefulness and sleep[2](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?67rlMk). However, establishing such correspondence is difficult because recordings often sample different circuits, and similar circuits may support different functions across recordings or within the same recording over time[3](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?lAu9dr).

A common workaround to this correspondence problem has been to use statistical measures that are less dependent on the specific brain regions sampled. Rather than comparing raw activity patterns directly, these approaches derive broader descriptors such as functional connectivity[4](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?HniMN0) and then summarize the resulting structure using scalar measures such as entropy[5](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?o7aoMK), metastability[6](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?hoF0nr), or small-worldness[7](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?HpP7HE). Although this facilitates comparison across experimental conditions, it comes at the cost of discarding fine-grained spatiotemporal structure, particularly information about how activity propagates through the brain. This is a significant limitation since behaviour requires distributed neural processes to be flexibly integrated and segregated[8](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?wlfmP0), which in turn demands that neural activity be dynamically routed across the brain[9](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?cBCAsF). The  routing of information is a universal phenomenon in both biological and manmade networks[10](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?A1WP8c), and this necessitates information flow to be coordinated across spatial, temporal and spectral scales[11](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?WTzzZF). A central unresolved challenge is therefore how to quantify these directed, multi-scale interactions in large-scale human recordings.

  

An alternative to coarse-grained summary measures is to use data-driven methods to identify low-dimensional representations of neural activity and compare recordings within a shared latent space[12](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?fqBwcL). Methods such as independent component analysis (ICA)[13–15](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Ert5tB) and dynamic mode decomposition (DMD)[16](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?zm9YaZ), and related latent-variable models[17,18](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?C3Stdx) extract modes or latent variables from high-dimensional recordings, onto which raw data can be projected to yield time-varying coefficients. In variance-ordered eigendecompositions such as PCA, lower-order modes often capture broader patterns of covariation, whereas higher-order modes reflect progressively finer spatio-temporall structures[8](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?BkEMab), and therefore capture patterns of co-activation across scales. Across multiple imaging modalities, including EEG[19–21,21](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?XdNCG0), MEG[22,23](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?0F14pz), fMRI[24–27](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?BMPj3b), and PET[28](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Qn7a4H), the spatial topographies of these eigenmodes have been shown to be stable across individuals and over time[29](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Awv7XM), allowing them to serve as a common basis for comparing multi-scale activity across recordings. However, these modes are typically derived from the signal amplitude or phase and therefore primarily describe patterns of co-activation rather than patterns of directed information flow. By contrast, recent work in MEG, ECoG, and EEG has shown that cortical activity exhibits rich patterns of directed flow on millisecond timescales, including spirals, sources and sinks, and planar flow. These flow patterns vary with pharmacological[30,31](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?mqZ3lh) and neuropsychiatric state[32,33](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?hL5725) and have been linked to diverse neural and physiological functions including perception[34–39](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?rTlnDR), memory[40–42](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?x69Aay),motor control[43](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Stkgc5), and arousal[44](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ln35mD). Yet it remains unclear whether these diverse flow patterns can be expressed in a common set of eigenmodes that are reproducible across subjects, behavioral states, and frequency bands, and therefore provide a basis for comparing activity flow across recordings. This question is particularly well suited to high-density EEG recordings in humans where fast-evolving patterns of activity can be resolved across the cortex with high spatio-temporal resolution.

Here, we introduce a basis for representing cortical activity based on eigenmodes of activity flow. We treat band-limited EEG as a continuous field on the 3-dimensional field on the surface of the scalp and infer instantaneous propagation direction from spatial gradients of oscillatory phase. We then apply differential operators to this vector field to extract patterns of divergence (source–sink flow) and vorticity (rotational flow), and perform eigendecomposition on these fields to derive divergence and vorticity flow eigenmodes. We show that these canonical flow modes are highly reproducible across subjects, behavioral states and frequency bands and remain largely intact in the presence of neurodegenerative disease. Individuals engaged in the same cognitive tasks elicited similar patterns of flow-mode activation suggesting that distinct flow modes have conserved functional roles across humans. Together, this approach provides evidence of a unified framework for describing, comparing, and modelling the brain’s macroscopic flow dynamics in health and disease.
