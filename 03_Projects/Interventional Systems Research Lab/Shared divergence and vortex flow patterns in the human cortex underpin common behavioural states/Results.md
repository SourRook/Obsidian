
## Deriving divergence and vorticity modes of activity flow 

We developed a computational strategy to derive and compare eigenmodes of activity flow from high-density electroencephalography (EEG) recordings across human recordings. At the core of this strategy is the computation of the unit phase vector field, which captures the instantaneous direction of neuronal activity propagation. The neural activity at each sensor yt is first decomposed into a set of band-limited signals (Fig. 1a i). These band-limited signals are then represented as nonstationary sinusoids At⋅sin (ϕt) , where the instantaneous amplitude At and phase t are obtained via the Hilbert transform. The spatial gradient of each phase time series, ∇ϕr,t is computed to yield a time-varying 3-dimentional vector field that encodes the local direction and rate of phase progression across the cortex. The phase gradient is then normalized at each spatial location to unit length, producing the unit phase vector field F(r,t)=r,t=∇ϕr,t/∇ϕr,t, which characterizes the direction of flow independent of the strength, akin to mapping the layout of roads throughout a city while ignoring the volume of traffic (Fig. 1a ii).

We hypothesised that a spatial basis derived from this activity flow would be naturally aligned across subjects, such that inter-subject differences are primarily expressed in the strength of flow along a shared set of spatial modes. To identify coherent patterns in the direction of signal flow in each subject we computed the divergence and vorticity of the unit phase field at each time point: Divr,t=∇∙F(r,t), and Vortr,t=∇×F(r,t), respectively. Divergence identifies net outflow (sources) or inflow (sinks) (Fig. 1a iii), while vorticity captures regions of rotational flow (clockwise or counterclockwise) (Fig. 1a iv) (see Arbitrary Manifold Vector Operators in Methods). 

To identify recurring flow motifs, we constructed time-by-sensor matrices of divergence and vorticity, where each row corresponds to a time point and each column to an EEG sensor (Fig. 1b i–ii). We then applied principal component analysis (PCA) separately to each matrix, extracting spatial patterns that capture the dominant modes of signal flow over time. This yielded two families of spatial flow eigenmodes: divergence eigenmodes d={d1,…,dn} (Fig. 1b iii) and vorticity eigenmodes v={v1,…,vn} (Fig. 1b iv), which we collectively refer to as flow modes. Each flow mode represents a unique pattern of activity flow—either source/sink (divergence) or rotational (vorticity)—that recurs across time at a particular spatial frequency. The flow modes are the eigenvectors obtained from PCA of the divergence/vorticity field. Their eigenvalues form a spectrum ordered by variance explained. Projecting the maps onto these flow modes yields a time series that reflect their activation over time, with the sign of the amplitude determining the direction of flow in the underlying vector field (Fig. 1c i). Transitions between positive and negative amplitudes, therefore, correspond to reversals in the direction of activity flow, capturing the brain’s capacity to reroute neural signals dynamically (Fig. 1c, ii). 

## Spatial topography of flow modes are reproducible across subjects 

To examine the flow dynamics of cortical activity, we recorded EEG with 127 effective electrodes in a cohort of healthy young adults (n = 19; mean age 23.0 ± 2.0 years, mean ± s.d.; 7 male) during an overnight sleep study76. We analysed two resting-state sessions: one in the evening prior to sleep (session 1) and one the following morning (session 2). Each session comprised 20 minutes of EEG—10 minutes eyes open, followed by 10 minutes eyes closed—during which participants passively listened to intermittent pink noise. For further experimental details see Healthy Young Cohort in Methods.

We first computed the group-level flow modes of α-band (8–12 Hz) brain activity during the morning session by concatenating the participants’ time series of divergence and vorticity maps (separately) before applying PCA. We focused on the α-band activity given its cardinal role in the human resting state77–79. Fig 2a and 2b show representative eigenmodes of vorticity and divergence, respectively. We found that a small subset of flow modes accounts for virtually all inter-individual variability in alpha-band flow dynamics (Fig. 2c i), with the first 20 vorticity modes explaining 91% of the cross-subject variance. In contrast, the first 20 divergence modes explain 96%. The evening session yields similar values of 91% and 96%, respectively (Fig. 2f i). We observed that vorticity flow modes were more excited at low orders compared to divergence modes (d1-3, largest P = 4.8x10-5) and suppressed at high orders (d>15 max P = 1.1 x 10-7) across both morning and evening sessions (Fig 2c ii and Fig 2f ii).

To examine the similarity in the EEG flow dynamics across individuals, we computed the flow modes for each participant at the two recording sessions (evening and following morning) and quantified their similarity to the group-level morning flow modes (reference family) using pairwise absolute Pearson correlations. Each flow mode represents a spatial pattern of weights across EEG channels, and correlations were computed between these channel-weight vectors, effectively comparing the topography of each subject’s flow modes with the reference flow mode family. Since the order of the flow modes can vary between participants, we first reordered the individual flow modes to align with the order of the reference family (see Kuhn–Munkres algorithm in Methods). We found that the first 20 divergence and vorticity modes were highly correlated across participants in both the morning session (Fig. 2d, d1-d20; V1-V20, P < 0.001) and evening session (Fig. 2g, d1-d20 ; V1-V20 , P < 0.001;) relative to a surrogate distribution (see Null Models for Mode-to-Mode Correlations in Methods), with lower order flow modes showing higher correlations. For example, in the evening recording, the absolute correlation of flow mode 1 across participants was d1 |r|  = 0.86 ± 0.062 (mean ± standard deviation, SD) and v1 |r| = 0.97 ± 0.008 in the divergence and vorticity fields, respectively, and that of mode 20 was d20 |r|  = 0.544 ± 0.041 and v20 |r| = 0.524 ± 0.057. Similarly, in the following morning recording, the correlations across participants was d1 |r| = 0.91 ± 0.039, v1 |r| = 0.97 ± 0.009,  d20 |r| = 0.47 ± 0.053, and v20 |r| = 0.62 ± 0.072. Using the same procedure, we also tested whether the modes were reproducible within participants by computing the spatial similarity between morning and evening sessions and found similarly high correlations, (for example: d1 |r| = 0.86 ± 0.062, v1 |r| = 0.97 ± 0.008, d20 |r| = 0.54 ± 0.041 and v20 |r| = 0.52 ± 0.057 (see Supplementary Materials Fig 1).

We next tested whether the coordination between the flow modes is also conserved across individuals. We constructed a cross-mode covariance matrix (CMC) for each participant by projecting the divergence and vorticity maps onto their respective flow modes and computing the covariance of the time series of activations between all flow mode pairs. The CMC captures how strongly flow patterns are coupled between different spatial frequencies, thereby providing a compact, subject-specific fingerprint of multiscale flow organisation. We then quantified the cross-participant cosine similarity between the CMCs and z-scored these values against a null distribution, obtained by independently permuting the elements of the CMC vector 5000 times (see Supplementary Materials Fig 1 and Cross-Mode Covariance in Methods). We found that CMCs exhibit high similarity across individuals for the morning session (Fig. 2e, divergence modes, t = 7.2, P = 9.9 × 10⁻⁷; vorticity modes, t = 2.4, P = 0.02) and again for the evening session (Fig. 2h, divergence modes, t = 8.9, P = 5.0 × 10⁻⁸; vorticity modes, t = 5.6, P = 2.4 × 10⁻⁵), demonstrating that the multiscale flow architecture of divergence and vorticity flow is conserved across subjects.

Spatial topography of flow modes are reproducible across multiple frequency bands during resting and task-evoked recordings 

We next asked whether the flow architecture we observed in young adults persists in older age and generalises to an active task state. We recorded high-density EEG in a healthy older cohort (191 electrodes; n = 28; mean age 77.4 ± 4.9 years, mean ± s.d.; 15 women) during 5 min eyes-closed resting state and a three-session auditory attention task in which sequences of spoken letters (B, K, and M) were presented while the participants were instructed to listen passively (P task), respond to the frequent letter (F task), or respond to the oddball letter M (O task). The session order was counterbalanced between participants. Structural MRI was also acquired but not used here. See Healthy Older Cohort and Alzheimer’s Disease Cohort in Methods for experimental details.

We first examined the flow dynamics during the resting state as before but extended the analysis to three bands—low frequency (2–8 Hz, LF), mid frequency (9–20 Hz, MF), and high frequency (21–35 Hz, HF). We computed the flow modes for each participant in each band and then quantified their similarity to a reference mode family, i.e., the group-level flow modes at LF, using pairwise absolute Pearson correlations as before. Fig. 3a shows the reference eigenmode family and individual participant family of vorticity eigenmodes at the LF band, and Fig. 3b shows the corresponding spatial correlation between the individual and the reference family (reference channel weights vs subject channel weights for each flow mode). As in the young cohort, the flow modes in the elderly cohort were also highly correlated across recordings. This finding was reproducible across all three frequency bands and for both the divergence eigenmodes (Fig. 3c, d1-d20, P < 0.001) and vorticity eigenmodes (Fig. 3d, V1-V20) relative to a surrogate distribution. 

We next compared flow modes between frequency bands within each recording. We found that the spatial patterns of both divergence (Fig. 3e, d1:50) and vorticity modes (Fig. 3f, v1:50) were highly reproducible between LF, MF, and HF bands. For example, vorticity mode 1 showed strong correlations across bands (LF v MF v1 |r| = 0.95 ± 0.02, LF v HF v1 |r| = 0.90 ± 0.037; ; MF v HF v1 |r| = 0.90 ± 0.038), while higher-order flow modes such as mode 25 remained significantly correlated, albeit at lower magnitudes (LF–MF: v25 |r| = 0.73 ± 0.050; LF-HF v25 |r| = 0.64 ± 0.054; MF–HF: v25 |r| = 0.72 ± 0.039, all P < 10-5, Bonferroni-corrected). These findings indicate that a single family of flow modes recurs across frequency bands.

We next tested whether the coordination between flow modes was reproducible across individuals. For each subject and frequency band, we computed a cross-mode covariance (CMC) matrix, capturing the temporal covariance between modes of different orders. We then quantified the similarity of these CMC matrices across participants for divergence (purple) and vorticity modes (green). CMC similarity was significantly greater than expected by chance across all frequency bands, indicating that the coordination structure between flow modes is largely subject and frequency invariant. CMC similarity was significantly greater than expected by chance across all bands: divergence modes, LF t = 10.22, P = 8.9 × 10-11; MF t = 12.17, P  = 1.8 × 10-12; HF t = 16.10, P = 2.3 × 10-15; vorticity modes, LF t = 18.41, P = 8.2 × 10-17; MF t = 18.09, P = 1.3 × 10-16; HF t = 17.52, P = 2.8 × 10-16 (Fig. 3g). These results indicate that the covariance structure between flow eigenmodes of different orders is both reproducible across recordings and across frequency bands within a recording.

Oscillatory activity is known to exhibit a frequency–scale relationship, with lower-frequency rhythms tending to support long-range coordination across distributed cortical regions, whereas higher-frequency activity is more short-range and spatially localized[45](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?yMH7um). Yet here we report a common set of divergence and vorticity modes underlies activity flow across frequency bands. We therefore asked whether the difference between global and local patterns of activity flow could be explained by shifts in the relative contribution of low- versus high-order modes, rather than by changes in the modes themselves. This distinction is important because it determines whether frequency-dependent dynamics reflect fundamentally different patterns of activity flow, or different expressions of a shared underlying system.

To test this, we matched flow modes across low- and high-frequency bands based on spatial similarity and compared their eigenvalues. We found that low frequencies preferentially engaged lower-order modes, which capture more spatially extended structure, whereas high frequencies preferentially engaged higher-order modes (Fig. 3h), which capture more localized patterns . Across all bands, lower-order modes were dominated by vorticity, while higher-order modes showed a greater contribution from divergence  (Fig. 3i). This suggests that the cortex uses the same underlying building blocks for organising activity flow across all frequencies, with differences in spatial patterns arising from shifts in their relative weighting of modes of different orders rather than from changes in the modes themselves.

## Activation of flow modes shapes human attentional function

We next examined the flow of neural activity during an auditory attention task (Fig. 4). We compared the flow modes of individual participants at the LF, MF, and HF bands across the entire task (by separately concatenating the sessions' time series of divergence and vorticity maps before performing PCA) and quantified the similarity to the same reference flow mode family as before (group-level resting-state LF flow modes). We found that the flow modes computed independently on the task were highly correlated to those computed during rest, with a higher absolute correlation at lower order flow modes, Fig. 4a (for example: LF v1 |r| = 80 ± 0.055, v25 |r| = 0.52 ± 0.047; MF v1 |r|  = 0.83 ± 0.047, v25 |r| = 0.50 ± 0.044; HF v1 |r| = 0.77 ± 0.044, v25 |r| = 0.48 ± 0.050), confirming that the canonical flow architecture persists across brain states (for divergence eigenmodes see Supplementary Materials Fig 2). However, task engagement shifted the flow architecture towards stronger activation of high-order modes, particularly at the high-frequency band (Fig. 4b). 

To test whether different behaviours activate district flow architectures, we examined single-trial mode activations under the different auditory attention rules (i.e., passive listening (P), respond to frequent (F) stimuli, and respond to oddball (O) stimuli) (Fig.4c i). Divergence and vorticity maps for each participant were projected onto the first 25 group-level flow modes (Fig.4c ii), and amplitude variance for each flow mode was computed in the 1-second window following stimulus onset, yielding a trial-by-mode amplitude variance matrix (Fig.4c iii). We observed that the mode amplitude variance distribution differed depending on the attentional rule, as is evident in the representative mode-amplitude distribution for each task instruction in Fig 4d.

To test whether different behaviours during the task elicited distinct flow architectures we trained a binary random forest classifier on the 25 mode-amplitude values per trial (see Classification of task session using random forests in Methods). We found that despite the identical auditory input, the task rule was accurately classified from the mode amplitude variance on single-trials (Fig. 4e). The highest AUC was achieved when using the HF vorticity eigenmodes (Passive vs Frequent = 73.3% ± 3.9%; Passive vs Oddball = 73.0% ± 3.8%; Frequent vs Oddball = 68.0% ± 4.5%) and for HF divergence eigenmodes (Passive vs Frequent = 64.4% ± 4.0%; Passive vs Oddball = 74.2% ± 4.1%; Frequent vs Oddball = 68.0% ± 3.8%, see Supplementary Materials Fig 2c). Participant performance was at ceiling across all three tasks (Passive: 97.5% ± 12.5%; Frequent: 98.2% ± 3.3%; Oddball: 97.5% ± 6.1%). Pairwise comparisons confirmed no significant differences (PvO: t = –0.006, P = 0.99; FvO: t = 0.51, P = 0.61; PvF: t = –0.28, P = 0.09), ruling out performance differences as a confounding factor.

  

We next asked whether the flow architectures evoked by the attentional tasks were consistent across individuals. To test this, we extracted feature-importance vectors from each participant’s three Random Forest classifiers (Passive vs Frequent, Passive vs Oddball, Frequent vs Oddball). Each classifier assigned an importance weight to every flow mode, yielding a vector that summarised which flow modes carry the most information for distinguishing between task instructions in that individual. To assess whether participants rely on the same subset of flow modes when attending to the same instruction, we compared feature-importance vectors across individuals in two ways: matched pairs, where the same model was compared between two participants (e.g., Alice–Model 1 vs Bob–Model 1), and mismatched pairs, where different models were compared across participants (e.g., Alice–Model 1 vs Bob–Model 2). For each pairwise comparison, we computed the absolute cosine similarity between the corresponding feature-importance vectors: values range from 0 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating a more substantial alignment. We found that, across all three models, matched comparisons consistently showed higher cosine similarity than mismatched ones (Fig. 4f). For vorticity eigenmodes, matched pairs in Model 1 exceeded Model 1 vs Model 2 (t = 3.2, p = 0.0033) and Model 1 vs Model 3 (t = 7.9, P = 1.6 × 10-8); matched pairs in Model 2 exceeded Model 2 vs Model 1 (t = 11.3, P = 1.0 × 10-11) and Model 2 vs Model 3 (t = 6.8, P = 2.8 × 10-7); and matched pairs in Model 3 exceeded Model 3 vs Model 1 (t = 4.6, P = 8.7 × 10-5) and Model 3 vs Model 2 (t = 3.7, P = 9.8 × 10-4), for results with divergence modes, see Supplementary Materials Fig 2). In other words, when two individuals attend to the same instruction, the flow of their cortical activity is routed more similarly than if they were attended to different rules. 

Spatial topography of flow modes are reproducible in Alzheimer’s Disease

Having established that the flow of cortical activity is moulded by a set of canonical flow modes that are conserved in healthy individuals, we next examined the flow dynamics under a disease condition in which the neural tissue is compromised due to neurodegeneration. We repeated the experiment in a cohort of participants with moderate Alzheimer’s disease (AD; n = 33, 18 females; age = 76.3 ± 7.8 years), matched in age to the healthy older cohort (no significant age difference, P = 0.51). The Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale - Cognitive subscale (ADAS-Cog)85 was 37.1 ± 12.1, suggesting moderate impairment. The patients showed reduced accuracy in the simple attention tasks relative to the healthy cohort (93.3 ± 7.6% vs. 97.8 ± 3.3%, t = –2.9, P = 0.0052, Supplementary Materials Fig 3). 

  

We applied the computational framework to the band-limited EEG to extract subject divergence and vorticity eigenmodes in AD and tested whether the modes were preserved relative to the healthy older cohort by computing the similarity of each participant’s flow modes to the reference mode family (group-level resting-state LF flow modes in the healthy older cohort). During the resting state, we found that the low and high order flow modes remained remarkably intact in the AD cohort (Fig. 5a,b). Resting modes across all frequency bands were highly correlated with the reference flow mode family in the healthy older cohort for both divergence eigenmodes (Fig. 5c) and vorticity eigenmodes (Fig. 5d), and these flow modes were also conserved between rest and task states (Supplementary Materials Fig 4).
