A central challenge in systems neuroscience is to identify shared representations that enable neural activity to be compared across recordings. A common approach is to derive shared low-dimensional representations—such as large-scale functional networks—that capture patterns of co-activation between brain regions. However, these representations do not capture how activity is routed through distributed circuits, which is critical for behaviour. Whether a shared representation of large-scale activity flow exists across humans remains unclear. Here, we report cortical activity flow in high-density electroencephalography (EEG) recordings is routed through canonical divergence and vorticity eigenmodes derived from the time-varying unit phase vector field. These flow modes exhibit a one-to-one correspondence across subjects, are reproducible across frequency bands in rest and task states, and remain preserved in the presence of neurodegenerative pathology. During an attention task, identical sensory input elicited distinct patterns of flow mode activation depending on task instruction, and individuals attending to the same instruction exhibited more similar activation patterns than those attending to different rules, suggesting that flow modes have similar functional roles across humans. Together, these findings identify divergence and vorticity modes as a shared basis for describing, comparing, and modelling macroscopic activity flow in the human brain.
