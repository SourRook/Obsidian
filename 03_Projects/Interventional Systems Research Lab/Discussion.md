

In this study, we show that human cortical activity flow can be expressed in terms of a common basis of flow eigenmodes, providing a framework for comparing activity across subjects, states, and frequency bands.Importantly, these flow modes were not idiosyncratic to individual participants. When individuals attended to the same task rule, their mode activation patterns were more similar than when they attended to different rules. This indicates that both inter-individual and state-dependent differences—such as rest versus task or different task rules—are captured by changes in the activation of a common set of modes, rather than by the emergence of new patterns of flow. These findings further suggest that these modes have reproducible functional roles in human behaviour.

To estimate activity flow, we used spatial gradients of the instantaneous phase, consistent with prior work[43,44,46,47](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?gMNfAe). By normalising phase vectors to unit length, this approach isolates the geometry of signal propagation independently of fluctuations in signal magnitude. While this enables recovery of the large-scale structure of cortical flow, it necessarily discards amplitude information that may also carry physiological relevance. Future work should therefore examine how flow architectures derived from unit phase fields compare to those obtained from unnormalised phase-vector fields.

A second limitation concerns the analytic separation of divergence and vorticity. Although these operators expose interpretable source–sink and rotational flow motifs, this separation between divergence and vorticity flow is, however, artificial. In some cases, divergence and vorticity modes appeared to show similar temporal dynamics, suggesting potential redundancy and an effective dimensionality lower than the number of reported modes. Future work using joint dimensionality-reduction frameworks across both divergence and vorticity mode families may yield more parsimonious representations of activity flow.

Finally, we identified flow modes using principal component analysis applied to concatenated divergence and vorticity fields. While PCA efficiently recovers reproducible patterns across individuals and datasets, it captures variance rather than statistical independence or dynamical structure. Alternative decompositions—such as independent component analysis[14](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?N7WBMe), dynamic mode decomposition[16](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?MtRlkC) or empirical mode decomposition[48](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?QPRjRO) may also work well.  

A central aim of systems neuroscience is to identify shared representations that capture how neural activity is coordinated to support cognition. Traditionally, macroscopic brain activity has been analysed in terms of networks, defined as sets of regions whose activity covaries over time. While this framework has been useful, it is inherently limited to pairwise relationships and does not capture the directed, higher-order interactions that arise in a highly interconnected, multi-scale systems[11,49,50](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?zDyAyd). An alternative approach has been to describe brain activity in terms of eigenmodes derived from structural or geometric properties, such as the connectome[51,52](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ci4CND) or geometry[53](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?BPT5UQ). These modes provide a basis for representing spatial patterns of activity and have been shown to approximate functional connectivity and task-evoked neural responses[53](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?6rsuo4). However, such representations remain fundamentally static and do not capture the direction of activity flow, which are central to how neural activity is dynamically coordinated over time.

Here, we extend this framework by introducing eigenmodes of activity flow derived directly from high-density EEG recordings. These flow modes provide a parsimonious basis for reconstructing the directed spatiotemporal dynamics of neural activity as predicted by neural field theory[54,55](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?56zzyp), importantly, defining a shared representation through which patterns of activity flow can be compared across subjects, frequency bands, and cognitive states.

A potential objection is that eigendecomposition yields a complete set of basis functions capable of reconstructing any smooth signal, and that the patterns we observe may therefore be mathematical artefacts. We address this concern in two ways.

First, the activation of flow modes is systematically related to behavioural state. When individuals attended to the same instruction during an attentional task, their mode activation patterns were more similar than when they attended to different instructions, despite identical sensory input. If the basis were arbitrary, there would be no reason for different individuals to recruit the same modes in a state-dependent manner. 

Second, divergence and vorticity modes showed differences in their eigenvalue spectra. Vorticity modes carried greater power at lower orders, whereas divergence modes dominated at higher orders, indicating that large-scale, long-range flow is primarily vortical, while more localized flow is characterized by source–sink flow. In addition, divergence modes were more strongly expressed at higher frequencies, whereas vorticity modes were more prominent at lower frequencies. Again, if the basis were generic, there would be no intrinsic reason for one family to systematically carry more or less power at higher orders or in one frequency over another. Similarly, the observation that task engagement preferentially up-regulate higher-order divergence modes—and suppress them at rest —is inconsistent with the set of basis functions being arbitrary. Rather this apparent functional dissociation suggests that the flow modes reflect genuine organisational principles of the cortex. 

  

Neural mass models have shown that activity flow patterns, including vortices, sources, and sinks, reconfigure during transitions between brain states[46](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?2fOEDz).. Intracranial recordings further demonstrate that the direction of activity flow can reverse between memory encoding and retrieval[42](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?q56lJ8). Here, rather than focusing on specific task-dependent reversals, we identify a general organizing principle: activity flow is structured by a set of canonical modes, each capturing a distinct spatial pattern of propagation across the cortex. Within this framework, changes in flow direction arise from systematic reweighting of these modes, and are observed across multiple cognitive contexts, including task switching and transitions between rest and task. These findings suggest that modulation of activity flow direction is not specific to memory, but reflects a general mechanism for reconfiguring brain-wide dynamics across cognitive states.

Classically low and high-frequency neural activity are thought to reflect global and local processing respectively, arising from two distinct mechanisms of long-range cortical interactions vs local excitatory–inhibitory interactions[56](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?r4ZwH4). Our results challenge this dichotomy. We show that neural activity at all frequencies is governed by the same canonical family of flow modes and that high-frequency neural activity can also form stable global flow patterns. These findings are consistent with recent intracranial recordings demonstrating that very high-frequency neural activity (100-400Hz) exhibits long-range spatial synchronisation patterns[57](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?90o4dt). We extend this principle to demonstrate that every frequency band has access to both globally integrative and locally segregative flow patterns, depending on the order of the modes engaged. The key distinction is that high-frequency activity more frequently recruits higher-order (segregative) flow, whereas low-frequency activity is dominated by lower-order (integrative) flow. 

Our results show that the dynamic activation of conserved flow modes provides a principled taxonomy for how spatial patterns of activity flow are flexibly integrated and segregated to support information processing in the brain. The observation that these modes are invariant across subjects, brain states, frequencies,  suggests that they may function as state variables for the human cortex.
