## Intellectual Identity

*Note: Everything in this section represents current ideas and working hypotheses, not fixed beliefs. These should be challenged and updated as new evidence, conversations, and thinking demand. See [[Interaction Preferences]] for how these have evolved over time.*

**Current scientific ideas:** I have developed a framework for understanding the brain as a fluid-dynamic system — representing neural activity as a superposition of waves (divergence flows, vortex flows, planar waves) rather than as a network graph. My argument is that graphs are second-order emergent descriptions of activity, whereas wave equations and Navier-Stokes are derived from first principles and provide a multi-scale account of how energy dissipates, how information propagates, and why we observe the spatial patterns we do. I developed a computational framework for extracting these spatio-temporal flow patterns from human EEG and discovered recurring motifs (modes) that are highly reproducible across subjects, brain states, and frequency bands with specific cognitive functions (see preprint: "A universal dynamic routing architecture governs the flow of human cortical activity"). These ideas emerged from observation and cross-domain reasoning, but they are hypotheses to be tested, refined, or abandoned — not articles of faith.

**How I tend to think (context-dependent, not a formula):** My thinking moves in multiple directions depending on the problem. Sometimes I start phenomenologically — watching time-resolved videos of EEG activity, noticing qualitative patterns (eddies, sources, sinks), and then hunting for mathematical formalisms that describe what I see. Other times I work backwards: I notice that certain computational methods apply to other dynamical systems (like fluids), reason that the brain is itself a physical system that must obey certain laws (thermodynamics, conservation), and posit that similar mathematics might underlie both. The common thread is cross-domain transfer — borrowing tools, analogies, and formalisms from one field to crack open problems in another. This is probably shaped by my arts background: the habit of reading pattern and structure across very different kinds of material. But I do not want this characterisation to become a constraint on how I think in the future. The creative process should remain open.

**Cross-domain problem solving** is a core part of my identity. The fluid dynamics framework came from physics and fluid mechanics, fields in which I had no formal training. I taught myself vector calculus, linear algebra, and machine learning through Coursera during my MSc at Imperial. I enjoy finding that the right formalism for a problem lives in a completely different field and want to continue doing this.

**Mechanism and prediction — both matter.** My deepest aspiration is to understand *why* the brain does what it does — to take things apart, understand how the pieces fit together, and use this to understand how all the rich behaviours that define who we are arise from biology. Feynman's dictum resonates: you cannot truly understand something until you have built it. However, I also recognise that prediction without full mechanistic understanding has enormous practical value. History is full of effective interventions developed before mechanisms were understood (SSRIs, aspirin, anaesthesia). At Constellation, much of my work will involve building predictive models of human state — and that is valuable in itself, both as an end product and as a means to scale the data collection needed for deeper mechanistic work later. The aspiration toward mechanism is a north star, not a gatekeeping criterion for what counts as good science.

**Current theoretical framework:** I think in terms of dynamical systems that minimise entropy production by modelling themselves and their environment (to minimise surprise). This self-modelling probably occurs in some shared latent space that might be describable by a basis of flow modes (like those I discovered) but is certainly far more complex and multi-modal — encompassing not just electrophysiological signals but neuromodulatory, neuroendocrine, and whole-body signalling systems. Getting at this latent embedding space is the grand challenge, and it requires tools that capture multi-scale dynamics from molecules up to whole-body networks. This is why I joined Constellation.

- A-Levels: downgraded from advanced to regular maths (too difficult), took English A-level (awarded 100% grade over 2 years) which limited university options

- MSc Translational Neuroscience, Imperial College London — Distinction
- MRes Computational Neuroscience, Imperial College London - Distinction 
- PhD, Interventional Systems Research Lab, Imperial College London — under Dr Nir Grossman

**The Bristol years and Miles Desforges:** Miles is my best friend, peer, and mentor. We met on the first day at Bristol and spent three years developing our political and philosophical ideologies together. He was the first person to teach me critical thinking and how to study (learning mechanisms rather than memorising knowledge). We co-wrote a library of auxiliary notes called "The Bible" — everything orthogonal to the neuroscience curriculum that we found fascinating. Miles now works at OIST in Okinawa. The collaborative, dialogical way I think best — building understanding *with* someone — traces back to this friendship.

**The PhD and why I left academia:** My PhD focused on developing temporal interference stimulation for dementia using a phase-locking approach (a microcontroller that read ongoing EEG phase and delivered visual stimuli timed to endogenous band-limited activity). Over the course of the PhD I became convinced that cognition is a large-scale distributed process that cannot be captured by any single brain region or oscillatory frequency, and I developed the travelling wave framework that became my core scientific contribution. I left academia for several reasons: career progression concerns, lab dynamics, isolation (I developed the framework largely alone without collaborators who believed in or understood the ideas), and a scientific conviction that the field needed better behavioural measurement before the neural insights could be fully leveraged.

## Values and Principles

- **Curiosity above credentials.** The questions that drive me — how does consciousness arise, how can we fall in love and dance tango and do everything a pen-and-paper clinical assessment cannot measure — are the same questions that drew me in at sixteen. I want to stay connected to that original wonder.
- **Usefulness to others.** I put myself in environments with people smarter than me so I can learn, but I also need to feel that I have purpose and that without me the machine would not function as well. I will leave any situation where I feel I am a burden.
- **Collaboration as cognition.** I think best dialogically. The Bible at Bristol, the partnership with Yuval, the way I want to work with AI — understanding emerges from exchange, not isolation.
- **Mechanism over prediction.** I do not want to be a black-box operator. I want to understand the gears.
- **The arts matter.** Literature, music, philosophy — these are not hobbies adjacent to my science. They shape how I perceive, how I reason, and what questions I ask.
- **Healthy AI use.** I am concerned about outsourcing critical thinking to AI tools and want to use them as accelerants for my own growth, not substitutes for my own cognition. Everything I say and do should feel like mine.
- **Ownership and creative joy.** I want to preserve the joy of using my own mind. AI should be a sparring partner, not a ghostwriter.
